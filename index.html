What is Jev?

Most AI models write. Jev, from TypeSafe AI, decides. You give it some text and a question with a fixed set of possible answers. It picks one and tells you how sure it is. That’s the whole job.

Because it never writes, it is fast (around a fifth of a second in published tests) and cheap enough to run on every message, claim or output instead of a sample. The catch: it won’t explain itself, so your code, and sometimes a person, decides what to do with the answer.

Want the deeper take? Sebastian Raschka argues it’s more than a classifier, and that how well it generalizes across tasks is the real story: It’s Easy to Dismiss Jev as Just a Classifier →

Good at

  • Routing to the right team
  • Yes/no policy and evidence checks
  • Rating against a rubric
  • Gating risky actions

Not built for

  • Writing or summarizing
  • Math or new values
  • Images or audio
  • Explaining its answer

The building blocks

Every question is one of three kinds

Jev’s makers call them Noul, Score and Choice. In plain words: a yes/no, a rating, or a pick from a list. You can ask several in one call, and each is answered on its own.

Why it matters

Cheap enough to check everything

Today most teams check a small sample of AI outputs, claims or tickets, because a large model reading every one costs too much and takes too long. Jev flips that. It grades all of them, settles the clear-cut cases on the spot, and hands only the uncertain ones up the chain.

That’s the pattern all three guides keep coming back to: Jev for the high-volume, closed questions; an LLM when someone needs the reasoning; a person when the decision really matters.

Honest caveat. Jev is in early access. Speed and cost figures come from vendor and partner tests, and TypeSafe doesn’t claim its probabilities are calibrated, so test thresholds on your own data before trusting them.

The three versions

Same brief, built in sequence

The three guides weren’t written in isolation. Each model worked from what came before, which is why some parts look alike and others don’t.

Codex Astra kept the first draft’s look (its stylesheet is the same) but wrote a new set of 100: five per industry across 20 industries, each marked as fictional.

Grok took Codex Astra’s intro, industry list and first three cases word for word, then wrote 97 shorter cases of its own in a lighter, JavaScript-built page.

First-notice-of-loss claim typing

Choice

Collision, glass, theft, water damage, or liability.

FNOL arrives by phone transcript, web form, or email. Jev picks the claim type and a second Choice for the coverage line. The claim is created in the right system with the right fields before an adjuster touches it.

Input
“Tree came down on my garage roof during last night’s storm, water is getting in.”
Ask
Choice [collision, glass, theft, water-damage, wind-hail, liability, fire]
Jev returns
wind-hail (0.88)
Next
Homeowners claim opened; catastrophe-event flag checked against the storm date.

Claims intake

Choice

Classify claim narratives into collision, theft, water damage, or other covered-event categories.

Claims intake can classify a narrative into a covered-event category before an adjuster reviews policy terms. Separating collision, theft, water damage, and other events helps select the right questions and supporting-evidence checklist.

Input
Fictional claim narrative: ‘A pipe under the kitchen sink failed overnight and water damaged the cabinet and floor.’ The intake record includes the reported date and room.
Ask
Which claim event category best matches the narrative? Choose one: collision, theft, water damage, other covered event, unclear.
Jev returns
Choice: water damage (illustrative)
Next
Start the water-damage intake checklist and have an adjuster confirm coverage, timing, and the reported property evidence.

Jev maps the free text to the fixed reason list so return analytics are consistent per SKU. Sizing complaints above a rate threshold auto-flag the product page for a size-guide update.

Ask
Choice [size-small, size-large, quality, not-as-described, changed-mind, damaged, wrong-item]
Jev returns
size-small (0.95)
Next
SKU sizing counter incremented; fit note suggested for the product page.

Phishing desk

Choice

Classify reported messages against predefined phishing indicators for analyst review.

An analyst receives a reported email with sender, subject, body excerpt, link text, attachment names, and a short reporting reason. Jev classifies the message against a fixed indicator taxonomy, helping the phishing desk prioritize analyst review while keeping the final disposition with a qualified investigator.

Input
Fictional report PH-204 says a message from billing-example.test asks for an urgent password reset, uses a mismatched link domain, and includes no approved campaign reference.
Ask
Does this reported message fit a phishing indicator category? Choice list: credential theft; malware delivery; impersonation; benign or unclear.
Jev returns
Choice: credential theft
Next
Send the category and supplied evidence to the phishing analyst, who verifies headers, links, and reporting context before any containment action.

Project management

Choice

Route requests for information to structural, architectural, mechanical, or electrical reviewers.

A project coordinator can send each request for information to the discipline that owns the answer, preserving a short audit trail of the question, drawing reference, and requested response date. This reduces forwarding loops while leaving technical interpretation with qualified reviewers.

Input
Fictional Harborline Clinic expansion RFI-014 cites drawing A-203, asks whether a wall assembly meets the partition detail, and attaches the RFI text plus discipline responsibility matrix.
Ask
Which reviewer should receive this RFI? Choices: structural; architectural; mechanical; electrical.
Jev returns
Choice: architectural
Next
Assign the RFI to the selected discipline queue, retain the drawing reference, and have the designated reviewer answer the technical question.
